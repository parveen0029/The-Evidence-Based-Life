# The Evidence-Based Life · Project Rules

The following rules apply to all work across this repository and must be strictly followed.

## Project Positioning
- This is a life guide ordered strictly by "return on investment" (ROI / cost-effectiveness). Inspired by the quantified style of `geekan/HowToLiveLonger`, but broader in scope: optimizing not just lifespan, but four core resources—lifespan, time & energy, money, and personal liberty; covering not just staying alive, but also personal finances, statutory law, career decisions, family, and parenting.
- Every single recommendation must answer two questions: what is spent (money / time / energy / willpower), and what is gained in return (change in all-cause mortality / reduction in specific cause of death / savings in time & energy / financial savings / legal safeguards & personal freedom).
- Ordering principle: Cost-effectiveness comes first, not topical category. Entries with near-zero cost and massive benefit appear first.

## Target Beneficiary Framework (Beneficiary Tiers)
- The entire book calculates ROI based on four tiers of beneficiaries, ranked by the expected probability that the benefit returns to you in the future:
  ① **Yourself**;
  ② **Spouse and immediate family** (parents, children, grandparents, grandchildren);
  ③ **Friends, colleagues, and other relatives**—reciprocal relationships where helping them out may yield reciprocal returns in the future;
  ④ **Strangers**—the lowest tier, but not zero: low probability of return, and you do not know their character, with additional downside risks of extortion, false accusations, or retaliation.
- Benefits across different tiers are never combined. When writing about Tier ④, downside risks must be presented alongside the benefits (false accusations, being dragged into legal proceedings, retaliation)—never write only the benefits, but also never write "ignore strangers entirely".
- Entries involving other people must be structured in this sequence:
  ① First explain what to do when this situation happens to yourself or family—this is the primary storyline and the core of the "Benefit" section;
  ② Next detail the differences when dealing with friends/colleagues vs. strangers, focusing on self-preservation actions and liability disclaimers (do not jump into deep water, do not physically intervene in violent fights, do not move injured persons carelessly, neither dial emergency numbers blindly nor break the law);
  ③ State clearly in the Notes section which tier this entry's beneficiary primarily falls into.
- The beneficiary tier does not alter the benefit magnitude rating (magnitude is determined mechanically from the entry's own "Benefit" section); it simply dictates whether the action is worth expending that cost, which belongs in the Notes.

## Explicit Process Costs for "The Law Is on Your Side" Entries
- Whenever an entry concludes that "the law supports you", "you are entitled to demand", or "a court will rule in your favor", writing only the final outcome without the procedural process equates the theoretical win rate with actual benefit. You must explicitly specify: whether a lawsuit is required, how long it takes (first-instance ordinary procedure takes at least 6 months and can be extended; summary procedure takes 3 months), and who pays legal fees (attorney fees are not among the litigation expenses listed in statutory court fee measures, and "losing party bears costs" does not automatically include them).
- Similarly, when citing an exemption or burden-of-proof rule to reassure the reader, clarify exactly what phase of the process it governs: governing the final judgment does not prevent you from being dragged into the dispute; governing civil liability does not protect against administrative penalties or criminal liability. Before writing, ask: "Is the risk governed by this statute the exact same risk the reader actually fears?"

## Evidence Grading (Mandatory for Every Entry)
- Grade A: Quantifiable evidence from meta-analyses, large prospective cohorts, or randomized controlled trials (RCTs), providing concrete figures (e.g., HR, RR, percentage reduction).
- Grade B: Supported by empirical studies but difficult to quantify, or evidence derived from small-sample / single studies.
- Grade C: Practical experience or broad consensus, lacking direct published literature.
- Controversial Grade A/B entries must be flagged as "Controversy:" and state the counter-evidence.

## Citation Rules
- Cite only primary literature: peer-reviewed journal papers (with DOI or PubMed links) and official agency reports (WHO, CDC, National Bureau of Statistics, etc.).
- Never cite secondary summaries like social media posts, public forums, or content aggregators.
- Numbers must be verifiable in the original text; if uncertain, leave them out and mark as "Pending verification".
- Do not fabricate DOIs or paper titles from memory; if the original paper cannot be retrieved, mark as TODO—never fabricate citations.

## Entry Format
Every entry follows a uniform structure:
- **Recommendation** (Single sentence, starting with an active verb)
- Cost:
- Plain English:
- Benefit:
- Evidence Grade: A / B / C
- Source:
- Notes: (Controversies, target population, caveats)
- Directly beneath the title line, include an HTML comment:
  `<!-- Cost Tags: Money=0|Low|High Time=Low|Med|High Willpower=No|Some|Yes Benefit=Low|Med|High Metric=Mortality|Money|Time|Freedom -->`
  Invisible in GitHub Markdown rendering, used by `index.html` to filter entries by cost dimensions; keep synchronized when adding or editing entries.
  Definitions:
  - Money: 0 = Zero cost or saves money; Low = Tens to hundreds of currency units or under that amount monthly; High = Thousands of currency units or ongoing significant expenditure.
  - Time: Low = Under a few minutes or along the way; Med = One-off multiple hours or weekly hour-scale; High = Daily ongoing commitment.
  - Willpower: No = Done once and for all; Some = Tweaking a single habit or tolerating minor discomfort; Yes = Ongoing battle against inertia.
- **All literature links belong in the "Source" field—never pile them in "Notes"** (2026-09-21 reader feedback on search cards asking "why are there so many links in the notes?"). This originated when entries were expanded across multiple revisions, each appending a DOI into the notes, eventually bloating notes to nearly 1,000 words. Supplementary evidence, counter-evidence, and alternative solutions **all belong in the "Source" field**. Notes should only state conclusions and numbers without parenthetical citations. At most one link may remain in Notes, and only if it points to a `docs/` long essay. Rule of thumb: `grep -c http` across all book notes; normal entries have 0 or 1 link, 2 warrants inspection, and 3 is definitely overcrowded. Notes exceeding 700 characters are also a signal to migrate details to a `docs/` essay or into Source.
- **"Plain English"** translates the statistical metrics in "Benefit" into 1–2 everyday sentences: Convert HR/RR/OR into "roughly X% higher / Y% lower", strip study populations and designs, leaving only the direction and magnitude that readers care about. Forbidden words: HR, RR, OR, CI, cohort, meta-analysis; forbidden to introduce numbers not present in "Benefit". **Forbidden to add unverified facts**: symptoms, factual claims, or mechanisms not in "Benefit" are strictly prohibited (e.g. blood donation claiming "pale face / feeling cold", cold showers claiming "going to work while sick"). **Plain English must be self-contained**: readers often read only this line, so do not evaluate an unmentioned claim (forbidden: "'XX' is not made up", "'XX' is true"). State it affirmatively. For Money and Freedom metrics, translate into "how much money you recover / what consequences you will face". Search cards render this as the prominent summary; if missing, it will not display.
- **The entire book must be readable by any adult without professional training on the first read** (2026-09-20 user decision: "I find some parts hard to follow, make it accessible, otherwise the barrier is too high"). Covers entry titles, Cost, Plain English, Benefit, Notes, and docs/ essays; **Source is exempt** so citations and statutory article numbers can be verified. Method: explain technical terms inline in everyday language while keeping the term ("excipient, meaning the non-active filling or coating in a tablet"); convert study jargon into actions ("observational cohort" -> "simply tracked over time without a control group"); convert legal jargon into consequences and actions. **Never delete or alter numbers, confidence intervals, population, or year descriptions in Benefit**—add inline conversions like "about 28% lower" while retaining original values. Benefit is the bedrock of verifiability. **Entry titles: only add clarifying words, never delete or swap words**, because cross-references rely on title words as anchors (`tools/check-refs.mjs`). Deleting words degrades anchored references into bare rule numbers.
- **Hard standards at the sentence level** (2026-09-21 reader feedback: "Some people say it reads like unreadable AI slop; make it accessible and simple, so a single complete sentence requires no external references or cognitive burden to understand", with explicit care for slower readers, elderly readers, and readers with disabilities). That day, all 552 rules and 4 long essays were rewritten under these standards, reducing average sentence length from 66 characters to 29, and sentences over 70 characters from 1,385 down to 64 (the rest being indivisible statutory quotes and English bibliography entries). Future additions and edits must follow these criteria:
  - **One sentence expresses only one thought**: Keep sentences around 30 words, maximum 50. Replace semicolons with periods, avoid dashes.
  - **Every sentence must have an explicit subject** (you, doctor, police, employer, court). Minimize passive voice.
  - **Unpack nested parentheses**: Any parenthetical clarification over 10 words should be its own sentence. Keep terminology explanations brief (e.g., `95% CI 0.50–0.63 (credible range)`).
  - **Every sentence must make complete sense on its own**: Cross-references must explain the context before the reference; the reference is only for further reading.
  - **Plain English: 2 to 4 sentences, under 120 words**: Keep direction and magnitude, rest stays in Benefit. Omit sample sizes and designs ("257 patients", "that group", "randomized"). Avoid telegraphic style (choppy phrases). Connect causal/contrast clauses with "because", "therefore", "but". No closing motivational or summary flourishes.
  - **Controversial notes must still begin with "Controversy:"**: `sync-stats.mjs` counts notes starting with this keyword.
- **Avoid AI clichés / fluff** (2026-09-28 issue #42: reader reported the book had "too much AI flavor"):
  - **Meta-narration**: "This rule only solves one thing", "This rule does not advise you whether to go or not", "This rule calculates... not money".
  - **Section-ending moralizing/flourishes**: "From the moment you sign up, this decision is irreversible", "Visa-free does not mean liability-free".
  - **Repeated filler connectives**: Overusing "that is to say" or "namely" multiple times in a single paragraph.
  - **Metaphors requiring translation**: "the other side of the fence", "a shield", "a change of skin".
  - **Telegraphic bullet-speak**: Breaking a single list into disconnected fragmented sentences.
  - **Empty intensifiers**: "It is worth noting that", "Essentially".
- **Revision philosophy prioritizes fidelity**:
  - Negations, scopes, conditions, stances, and primary/secondary judgments are core content, not fluff. Keep constraints intact.
  - Sentence conclusions and actionable advice stay; only rhetoric is cut.
  - Layman translations following statutory quotes must be preserved.
  - Do not swap AI tone for bureaucratic jargon.
  - Do not make statements more specific or absolute than the source; when in doubt, keep the original wording.
  - Causal / contrast sentences can use "because", "therefore", "but" while staying concise.
- Benefit and Metric fields determine whether an action is worth taking (orthogonal to evidence grades, which evaluate credibility). Metrics: Mortality (health endpoints, survival), Money (benefits, entitlements), Time (energy, hours), Freedom (liability, personal data). **Never compare across different metrics**.
- Benefit magnitude is assigned mechanically from "Benefit" thresholds:
  - Mortality: relative reduction (≥20% High / 10–20% Med / <10% or surrogate endpoints Low)
  - Money: amount (10,000+ High / hundreds to thousands Med / tens Low)
  - Freedom: consequence (avoiding criminal liability High / avoiding detention or administrative penalty Med / avoiding civil dispute Low)
  - Time: savings (daily hours High / weekly hours Med / one-off Low).
- Syncing statistics: When entry counts or Grade A counts change, four places must be updated: README (text counts and 2 badges), index.html (5 descriptions, numberOfPages, header entry counts), CLAUDE.md directory summary, and `tools/og.html` + regenerating `og.png`. Run `node tools/sync-stats.mjs` for one-click synchronization.
- **Diff inspection of `docs/citation-cross-reference.md` after adding or deleting entries**: `sync-stats.mjs` runs `tools/check-refs.mjs`. If a rule number hasn't changed but the target title changed, it's a shifted citation. CI runs `node tools/check-refs.mjs --check`.
- **Every cross-reference must have an anchor**: Text surrounding the reference must match a word in the target title. Bare numbers are forbidden. Relative references ("see next rule", "see previous rule") are forbidden.
- **Distinguish statutory article citations from book rule citations**: `check-refs` checks quotation marks, brackets, law names, etc. Statutory citations must carry their statute name.
- **Scope of citation checking**: `book/` body + chapter intros + `docs/` long essays.
- **Cost-effectiveness tiers**: Not written into README, computed dynamically by `index.html` based on benefit magnitude + 3 cost dimensions.

## Directory Structure
Chapters are split into `book/01-*.md` … `book/34-*.md` (originally split into 31 chapters on 2026-09-08 because a single 531 KB file exceeded GitHub's 512 KB Markdown rendering limit). README contains the introductory guide, glossary, and table of contents; new or edited rules go into the corresponding book file; `index.html` reads the file list from README, then fetches them concurrently. The first line of each chapter file is a link back to the table of contents, line 2 is blank, line 3 is `# N. Chapter Title`.

In the README "Questions This Book Answers" table, **each chapter occupies exactly one row** (2026-09-19 user decision: duplicate links force readers to pause twice on the same destination with no logical distinction). If a chapter covers broad topics, string several questions with commas and end with a question mark (e.g. Chapter 19: "How are overtime and annual leave calculated, how much compensation is owed upon layoff, and how are workplace injuries certified and compensated?"). Do not split into two rows.

Digital editions: Three builds generated automatically by `.github/workflows/book.yml` on push to main, published to the fixed release `epub-latest` (download URLs remain stable; README links to them); PRs only build and validate without publishing; all build artifacts go to `dist/` (git-ignored). **Build artifacts are not committed to the repository** to avoid repository bloat and outdated binaries. Structure parsing and file listings live in `tools/lib/book.mjs`, shared across all three builds. **When adding a new docs essay, manually link it in four places**:
① The "Long Essays" cell in README top navigation table (must be markdown `[x](docs/x.md)`);
② The "Long Essays" line at the end of README;
③ The corresponding chapter TOC line in README (`Long essay: [docs/x.md](docs/x.md).`);
④ The `class="doc-links"` line in `index.html` (percent-encoded URL).
Validation: `grep -c "docs/<name>.md" README.md` must equal 3, `grep -c "<name>" index.html` must equal 1.
- EPUB: `tools/epub/build.mjs` (marked dependency, zip bundled internally), validated in CI with epubcheck. Local: `cd tools/epub && npm ci && npm run build`.
- Single-File Offline HTML: `tools/offline/build.mjs` (zero dependencies) inlines the text and docs essays into `window.__CORPUS__` inside `index.html` (`parts` for book/, `docs` for essays). Double-click to open, works offline. Essays open in an in-place modal on the web reader without redirecting to GitHub.
- PDF: `tools/pdf/build.mjs` + `tools/pdf/template.typ`, compiled via Pandoc to Typst, formatted in A4 with chapter page breaks, running headers, TOC with page numbers, and bookmarks down to entry level. Requires Pandoc ≥ 3.1 and Typst ≥ 0.13. Layout changes are made in `template.typ`.

## Writing Rules
- **Rules are written for the reader, not responding to an inquirer** (2026-09-21 feedback: user caught an entry titled "'Is rote memorization necessary?' is the wrong question" in Ch. 23 Rule 19, noting "You wrote this for me, the reader never asked that"). User prompts dictate what topic to cover, but the prompt itself must not enter the text—readers do not possess that context. After drafting, evaluate the title against three tests: ① Does it begin with an active verb offering concrete advice? ② Is it fully comprehensible without this conversation thread (phrases like "'XX' is the wrong question", "In reality it is not so", "Many assume" are warning signs)? ③ Does it prescribe an action, or merely evaluate a claim? Check the opening and closing sentences of Plain English and Notes as well—avoid concluding with "So what should be asked is not...". That entry was rewritten as "Design self-test questions that match future retrieval contexts rather than rereading material verbatim", stripping rhetorical commentary from Plain English and Notes.
- **Negative entries must target the specific misconception readers actually believe, and harms must be in the Benefit section, not hidden in Notes** (2026-09-21 feedback: Ch. 6 Rule 26 Plain English originally claimed "'eating breakfast aids weight loss' is backwards", to which the user responded "To a normal person, eating more food causes weight gain, that's not surprising; but what about whether skipping breakfast long-term is harmful? That wasn't addressed"). Two distinct issues: ① **Losing the target**: "People who eat breakfast weigh more" is intuitive since extra meals add calories; the meta-analysis actually debunked the claim that "skipping breakfast causes compensatory overeating at lunch/dinner that exceeds the saved calories", with the key finding being that breakfast skippers consumed 259.79 kcal less overall per day. Before drafting a negative entry, identify the exact claim readers believe, and verify whether the data directly refutes it. ② **The reader's primary concern belongs in Benefit**: Per project rules, Plain English can only draw from Benefit; placing the long-term harms of skipping breakfast in Notes keeps that critical finding hidden from prominent view. Benefit sections can adopt a multi-tier structure: first refute the popular myth, present the alternative approach, and address long-term risks.
- **Before judging an entry as "prohibiting without offering a solution", confirm whether the person truly lacks alternatives—do not mistake high switching costs for a lack of choice** (2026-09-21 feedback: I recommended omitting the night-shift rule on the grounds that "readers cannot avoid night shifts, presenting risks without solutions"; the user countered: "Readers can choose whether to take the job; some may switch jobs for their health, which is better than being in the dark"). Changing jobs, requesting reassignments, or factoring health into career choices are real decisions; high cost does not mean zero choice. Dose-response figures (each 5 years of night shifts adds 7% cardiovascular risk) provide empirical justification for transitioning early. Such topics follow the "dissect costs without imposing conclusions" template (Ch. 10 marriage, Ch. 18 childbearing): the Cost section specifies both high-cost and zero-cost options, Notes explicitly state "does not prescribe that everyone must leave night shifts", and point to actionable mitigations.
- Write in clear, idiomatic English.
- Restrained tone, non-preachy, zero exclamation marks.
- Maintain strict separation across metrics: mortality, time/energy, money, personal freedom. No cross-metric conversions.
- Plain language in body text: avoid abbreviations like ACM in favor of "all-cause mortality"; mandatory statistical abbreviations (HR, RR, CI, RCT) are explained in the README glossary, add new terms to the glossary before use; statutory clause numbers belong in Source, while body text focuses on concrete actions.
- Single entries should not exceed 10 lines; long analyses belong in dedicated files in `docs/`.
