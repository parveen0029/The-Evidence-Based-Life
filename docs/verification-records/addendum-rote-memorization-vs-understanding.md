# Addendum: "Should You Rote Memorize?" · Verification Record (2026-09-21)

Task Origin: A reader asked "Is there any content added about whether one should engage in rote memorization?"

Original Coverage: Section 23 Rules 14 to 18 (added the same day in a parallel session) covered **how to learn** — closed-book self-testing, spacing out study across multiple days, avoiding highlighting and repeated rereading as core strategies, interleaving different problem types, and that learning styles lack evidence. None of these rules directly answered **whether one should memorize**. Furthermore, Rule 16 is easily misinterpreted: what it rejects is repeated passive input such as "rereading and copying text," not "memorization" per se. Everyday discussions of "rote memorization" conflate two different meanings (repeated input vs. active retrieval); without teasing them apart, the question cannot be answered clearly.

Placement: Added 1 new rule at the end of Section 23 (Rule 19), requiring no rule number shifts. There are 3 cross-references within the section, all originating from this new rule pointing to Rules 14, 15, and 16; the diff in the cross-reference comparison table contains only these three new lines without displacing existing anchors.

## Section 23 Rule 19

| Reference | Verified | Figures / Data |
|---|---|---|
| Pan SC, Rickard TC (2018). Transfer of test-enhanced learning: Meta-analytic review and synthesis. Psychological Bulletin 144(7):710-756. <https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151> (Full abstract retrieved via Europe PMC, PMID 29733621) | Yes | 67 published and unpublished articles, 122 experiments, 192 transfer effect sizes, N=10,382; under random-effects model relative to "re-exposure without testing" control, d=0.40 (95% CI 0.31–0.50). Strongest transfer: across test formats, application and inference questions, medical diagnostic problems, mediator and related-word cues; weakest: rearranged stimulus-response pairings, material seen during initial study but untested, worked-example problems. Moderating factors: response format alignment, elaborative retrieval practice, initial test performance. Following publication bias adjustments using PET-PEESE and selection models, effect sizes of moderating factors were minimally altered, but intercept predictions were substantially depressed, "often indicating no positive transfer when none of the aforementioned moderators are present" |

Rated A: Meta-analysis with verbatim verifiable effect sizes and confidence intervals, including built-in publication bias corrections. Benefit magnitude "Moderate", calibrated to time metric — it does not reduce time spent, but increases output per unit time, consistent with the calibration of Rules 14, 15, and 16 in the same cluster. Cost perseverance is rated "Some": what changes is the method of practice, not the sheer volume.

The conclusion supported by evidence is **retrieve information in the format in which it will ultimately be used**, not a false dichotomy between memorizing and not memorizing. The section where the intercept is depressed must be included in the benefit column — it provides the direct rationale showing that when you "memorize verbatim with mismatched practice and testing formats," transfer can drop to zero; omitting it would lead readers to believe that "anything memorized can be applied."

**Title reworked once (same day)**: The initial draft was titled "The question 'should you rote memorize' is misframed: memorize what needs memorizing, but in the format you will use it." The user immediately pointed out that this answered the interlocutor rather than offering advice to the reader — readers do not hold that prompt in hand, and "misframed" has no anchor. Project formatting requires titles to begin with an actionable imperative verb. It was revised to "Test yourself on memorized material using the format of future applications, rather than settling for verbatim repetition." The concluding sentence in Plain Terms ("So the question is not whether to memorize, but in what format") and the remark ("Everyday criticism of 'rote memorization'... is well-deserved") were deleted together, as both spoke to the prompter with an evaluative tone. Benefit column, evidence level, and sources remained unchanged.

Encountered a bug in `tools/check-refs.mjs` when editing remarks: the script skips references where terms like "law", "measures", or "regulations" appear in the preceding 16 characters (assuming they refer to statutory article numbers). In Chinese, the reworked remarks had words like "method of study" (xuefa) and "method of practice" (zuofa) which contained the character "fa" (law), causing three intra-section references to be silently swallowed and disappear from the cross-reference table, while `--check` reported a false pass because no references were detected. Discovered only when the table was short by three lines. After rephrasing to neutral reference patterns, all 423 references were restored. Because Section 23 focuses on study methods, such phrasing patterns are frequent and must be deliberately avoided when drafting references in this section.
