# Section 2 Rule 26 Supplement: Tea Mycotoxins and Green Tea Extract (2026-09-23)

Task Origin: GitHub issue #27 (ccpfucker), titled "Objection to the view that drinking tea benefits health". The original issue argued: "Drink plain water and imported pure milk whenever possible; avoid drinking tea," citing potential mycotoxin contamination of tea leaves and hepatotoxicity of high-dose concentrated green tea extracts, concluding that "drinking tea is beneficial to health" cannot serve as a generalized claim and that tea should simply be treated as an ordinary beverage. Three references were attached.

## Verbatim Verification of the Three References

| URL | Verified | Key Findings from Abstract |
|---|---|---|
| <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2020.111830> (Cui P et al., 2020, Food and Chemical Toxicology; PMID 33127496) | Yes (Europe PMC abstract) | Testing 4 aflatoxins across 158 domestic post-fermented dark tea samples: "Two out of 158 samples were positive… (occurrence rate 1.27%)". Upper-bound exposure in high-exposure populations was 9.19 × 10⁻⁶ μg/kg/day, "lower than the JECFA acceptable value of 1.0 ng kg⁻¹ day⁻¹ on liver risk"; 95th percentile probabilistic carcinogenic risk was "equally below the acceptable carcinogenic risk level" |
| <https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins14070452> (Zhou H et al., 2022, Toxins; PMID 35878190) | Yes (Europe PMC abstract) | Testing 16 mycotoxins across 352 domestic tea samples: "Average concentrations of almost all mycotoxins in tea samples were below the established regulations, except for ochratoxin A in the dark tea samples". ## Disposition Decisions

**No changes to conclusions, evidence rating, or title.** The conclusions of all three cited papers confirm that "there is no risk at normal dietary tea consumption levels": the first demonstrated exposure below JECFA acceptable limits, the second explicitly stated no dietary mycotoxin risk from tea consumption in the Chinese population, and the third explicitly showed no hepatic adverse events with traditionally brewed tea. None of these refute the mortality association reported in China-PAR; the issue's conclusions reach far beyond the evidence it cited. The recommendation to substitute tea with "imported pure milk" provided no empirical backing and was rejected.

**Valid nuances incorporated into remarks.** Readers may understandably harbor concerns regarding moldy tea, and some consumers indeed use green tea extract supplements as a surrogate for "drinking tea" — nuances the rule previously omitted. The remarks were updated with two paragraphs detailing mycotoxin detection data alongside risk assessments, and differentiating concentrated extracts from brewed tea, concluding: "This rule applies exclusively to brewed tea; do not substitute it with concentrated extract supplements." All three citations were formally entered into the Sources column according to protocol, leaving the remarks free of external URLs.

**"Discard tea that has become damp and moldy" is a common-sense reminder (Level C rubric) lacking a standalone citation.** The issue noted that storage conditions affect safety, but none of the three papers provided quantitative thresholds regarding household storage; therefore, the actionable instruction is included without arbitrary fabricated numbers.

**The "Controversial" prefix is retained, and magnitude remains untouched.** The Benefit column was unchanged, requiring no adjustments to Plain Terms.
