# Addendum: Stop Comparing Yourself to Others and Stop Caring About Their Approval (Four Rules) · Verification Record (2026-09-19)

Task Origin: A user suggested: "It feels like something is missing — do we have rules on not comparing oneself to others and not stressing over others' evaluations? Would you recommend adding them?" Following the principle that "examples are not questions," the topic was abstracted to the general query: is there coverage in the book for mindset-related issues around "comparing with peers" and "fixating on how others view you"?

Original Coverage (Searching the entire book for "comparing with others", "social comparison", "social media", "Moments / feed", "others' evaluations", "self-esteem", "envy", "face / vanity", "showing off"): Only three rules touched on related topics, none directly targeting comparison: Section 6 Rule 22 (materialism and subjective well-being; shopping to regulate emotions), Section 3 Rule 14 (rumination), Section 4 Rule 10 (time caps on short videos and screen time; focused purely on total duration, not emotional dynamics). "Comparing with others" and "caring about others' evaluations" had zero dedicated rules.

Placement (Without altering table of contents structure, all were appended to the ends of existing sections without renumbering):
- Section 3 Rule 21 (Upward online comparison + RCTs on deactivation/time limits, Time metric, Level A)
- Section 3 Rule 22 (Spotlight effect and the "liking gap", Time metric, Level B)
- Section 3 Rule 23 (Socially prescribed perfectionism and suicidal ideation, Mortality metric, Level B)
- Section 6 Rule 23 (Do not inflate your budget just to move up a tier relative to peers, Money metric, Level B)

Incidental adjustments across three locations: Section 3 introductory note originally read "the final rule addresses expectations when dealing with institutions"; since the final rule changed, it was updated to "Rule 20," and an explanatory sentence was added noting that Rules 15 and 23 use the mortality metric; the cross-reference in Section 3 Rule 19 remarks ("see Section 1 Rule 16") was an outdated rule number (Section 1 Rule 16 is now HPV vaccine, while the 12356 helpline is Rule 25), which was corrected; Section 4 Rule 10 and Section 6 Rule 22 each added a sentence pointing to the newly added rules.

## Determination of Benefit Tiers (Where numbers do not mechanically map to thresholds, rationale is documented here)

- Section 3 Rule 21 = Large: Time metric based on time saved; RCTs directly demonstrate 60 minutes saved per day, meeting the "daily hours-level" threshold.
- Section 3 Rule 22 = Small: Time metric; lacks measurement of actual time saved, altering only a one-time cognitive estimate, rated "Small" under the one-time adjustment rubric.
- Section 3 Rule 23 = Small: Thresholds for mortality metric require relative mortality reductions; this rule provides no mortality drop, only correlations with suicidal ideation and suicide attempts (both surrogate endpoints), rated "Small" under the "surrogate endpoint only" rubric.
- Section 6 Rule 23 = Moderate: Money metric would ordinarily rate Large based on gross outlay (changing homes or cars costs tens of thousands of yuan), but financial savings here lack empirical quantification of "spending X yields Y return"; outcome variable is self-reported well-being rather than cash, following the "Moderate" rating of Section 6 Rule 22 (which likewise dissuades consumption based on subjective well-being evidence).

## Methodological Trade-offs

- **Avoided empty sloganeering like "Don't compare yourself to others" or "Stop caring what people think"**: Every rule in this book must answer what is spent and what is gained; empty slogans carry neither cost nor benefit. All four rules were grounded in verifiable numbers and concrete actions: set usage limits / delete apps, divide social estimates by two, treat specific intrusive thoughts as symptoms, and ask whether a budget increase buys the asset itself or merely positional standing.
- **No new chapter created**: Changing the table of contents structure requires explicit user approval; these four rules fit naturally within the existing frameworks of Sections 3 and 6.
- **No cross-metric aggregation**: Rules 21 and 22 belong to the time (and mental energy) metric, Rule 23 to mortality, and Section 6 Rule 23 to money; they are not pooled or compared against one another.
- **Separating correlation from causation**: The meta-analysis on upward social comparison provides correlational evidence (authors note longitudinal evidence is not stronger than cross-sectional, urging more longitudinal trials); the deactivation and screen-time limit studies are RCTs. They are presented separately in the text, with Level A justified solely by the latter.
- **Reporting costs alongside benefits in the text**: Deactivating Facebook for 4 weeks simultaneously reduced news knowledge by 0.19 SD; this belongs to the cost side of the rule and cannot be buried.
- **External validity boundaries documented in remarks**: The three trials and two laboratory experiments were conducted exclusively on US undergraduates and Facebook users; no comparable empirical data exists for Chinese platforms and demographics. The spotlight effect was demonstrated in wearing T-shirts and interacting with strangers; generalizing directly to workplace performance reviews lacks direct evidentiary backing.
