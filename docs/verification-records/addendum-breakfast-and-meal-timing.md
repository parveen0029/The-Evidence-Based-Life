# Added: Breakfast and eating time · Verification record (2026-09-21)

Source of the task: The user asked "Is there any content about whether to have breakfast, whether it is better to eat on time or when you are hungry?"

Original coverage: Search the whole book for "breakfast", "breakfast", "three meals", "eating on time", "fasting", "fasting", "eating window" and "limited time". Only Section 28, Article 1 (Do not use extreme dieting, fasting or vomiting to control weight) is relevant. It talks about eating disorders and does not answer "should you eat breakfast or when?" The two references to "three meals" in verse 29 refer to someone watching the meal after a bereavement. This topic has zero coverage.

Landing point: Section 6, Article 26, appended at the end of the section. The reason I put the negative list is because what I want to delete are two popular sayings ("Eating breakfast is good for health and weight control" and "16:8 light fasting is more effective than simply eating less"), not the eating itself. The two statements are combined into one sentence because they are two sides of the same mistake: they both think that "when to eat" can play a separate role from "how much to eat"; split into two statements, readers will easily only read half of it, and instead go away from "then I will try to change it to 16:8". Caliber recorded mortality (health endpoints vs body weight).

## Rating and selection

- **Rework on the same day on 2026-09-21 (user pointed out two problems)**: The title of the first draft reads "Don't force yourself to eat breakfast for the sake of health, and don't expect 16:8 light fasting to be more effective than simply eating less." In human words, the beginning is "'Eating breakfast helps control weight' is the opposite." The user's original words were, "For normal people, it is normal to gain weight after eating breakfast. I have not written about the harm of not eating breakfast for a long time." Both points are true: ① The target is lost. "People who eat breakfast weigh more" is not counter-intuitive to readers. Eating more meals will certainly lead to higher intakes. What the BMJ article really refutes is the popular statement "skipping breakfast will lead to overeating at lunch and dinner, and the total daily intake will be more." The key figure is that the total daily intake of the group who skipped breakfast was 259.79 kcal lower, which means they did not make up for it. The speaking and income columns have been changed to set this target first, and the intake item has been put before weight. ② "Is there any harm in skipping breakfast for a long time?" is the core question for readers. In the first draft, the entire paragraph was placed in the notes. According to the rules of the book, only things in the profit column can be used to talk about people. That sentence cannot be written in the most conspicuous position. The two observational articles (Zhang 2025, Rong 2019) have been moved from the remarks to the income column and the source column, leaving only mixed explanations in the remarks. The title was also changed to "Don't expect breakfast or 16:8 light fasting to help you control your weight. Choose a meal time that you can stick to for a long time." The mutual reference anchor point of Section 2, Item 34 was changed accordingly. Therefore, the income column becomes a "three-part": first refute the popular statement, then look at 16:8, and finally answer the long-term harm.
- **2026-09-21 Second rework (the user cited two folk sayings)**: The user's original words were "I read online that if you don't eat breakfast, your intestines will digest feces, which is equivalent to eating feces, and some people say it is bad for the stomach." Verification results: ① No corresponding mechanism for "intestinal digestion of feces" can be found in the literature. It is normal physiology for the colon to recycle water and electrolytes, regardless of whether breakfast is eaten, and feces will not be absorbed as food. This statement is not included in the main text - there are no "rumor-refuting" entries in the book (see writing rules: entries are written for the readers, not the people who respond to the questions), just state directly what should be checked. ② "Not good for the stomach" is half true and half false. The true part is not in the stomach but in the gallbladder. Remarks have been added (see the two tables below). At the same time, the original comment "Taking both sides together, the impact of meal time is very small, don't worry about it" is judged to be overdone: three independent observational evidences for cardiovascular disease, gallstones, and gastrointestinal cancer all point in the same direction. Although they cannot prove cause and effect, they should not be overshadowed by the sentence "the impact is very small." It has been changed to say separately - in terms of weight control, the impact is very small; there is only correlation evidence on whether skipping breakfast at all for a long time is good, and the direction is the same but not good. If you want to be safe, eat normally, but don't expect it to lose weight. At the end of the sentence, "but no difference can be seen in the total mortality rate" was changed to "no difference in the total mortality rate" was changed, and the "but" that sounded like comfort was removed.
- **Two new articles added in this round (notes are included in both, not in the income column)**:
  - <https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1521707> (Sun T, Zhang L, Lu Y, et al. Front Nutr 2024;11:1521707, PMID 39737153). Original text: "A total of 6,547 participants were included. The fully adjusted model indicated a positive correlation between the FMT of the day and the prevalence of gallstones (odds ratio [OR] = 1.05, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.02–1.08)""The risk of developing gallstones was the greatest when the FMT was between 09:00 and 14:00 (OR [95% CI] = 1.49 [1.24–1.77])". FMT = first meal time. **This is a cross-sectional survey and cannot determine the direction of cause and effect**. The remarks indicate that the eating habits may have changed after the gallbladder problem first occurred.
  - <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-023-08094-7> (Liu T, Wang Y, Wang X, et al. J Gen Intern Med 2023, PMID 36869181, Kailuan cohort ChiCTR-TNRC-11001489). Original text: "During a median follow-up of 5.61 (5.18–6.08) years, 369 incident GI cancer cases were identified.""Participants who did not eat breakfast had an elevated risk of esophageal (HR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.05-7.03), colorectal (HR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.34-4.01), liver (HR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.23-4.71), gallbladder, and extrahepatic bile duct cancer (HR = 5.43, 95% CI: 1.34-21.93)." Note: Only the colorectal and gallbladder bile ducts are cited, and the range 1.34–21.93 is written as it is, so that readers can see for themselves that there are too few events.
- **Checked but not included**: <https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-025-01223-4> (Ostadsharif N et al., J Health Popul Nutr 2026, PMID 41527009), meta-pool of 11 studies, 21,220 people, "higher meal frequency was associated with a reduced risk of FD (n = 2, OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.32-0.86)". Only 2 studies support this, and FD (Functional Dyspepsia) is a symptom diagnosis, and there is a high possibility of mutual causation with "number of meals". The evidence is too thin and has not been collected.
- **Definite A**: The three main pieces of evidence in the text are all randomized controlled trials (a meta-set of 13 trials + two RCTs, one of which was followed for 12 months), and the numbers can be verified verbatim.
- **Earnings are determined as "small"**: This article excludes two invalid practices. Readers who follow the instructions will get zero, not a decrease; according to the threshold of "only alternative end points are recorded as small". All three costs are zero, and the price/performance ratio is average (lower returns).
- **Marked "Controversy", and the original documents are given on both sides**: The observational side (skipping breakfast is 17% higher in cardiovascular disease, and the cardiovascular death is 49% higher) and the time-restricted eating side (an additional 1.46 kg can be lost under isocalorie conditions) are opposite to the conclusions of the main text, each written in a note and accompanied by a DOI.
- **Specifies that all-cause death in NHANES III was not significant**: HR 1.19 (0.99–1.42) across 1. When reporting this study online, they generally only quote 1.87 for cardiovascular death and omit the all-cause item, so the two numbers are written side by side in the notes.
- **How to reconcile the two sides of time-restricted eating**: The meta (30 trials, mostly short-term) said there was additional benefit, the longest and most rigorous single trial (NEJM 12 months) said there was no. The remark is written as "little impact on timing", not "completely ineffective".
- **Not written**: Inference on the mechanism of eating time and circadian rhythm (the so-called chrono-nutrition that eating early is better than eating late), insufficient evidence for human endpoints, so it has not been included; there is no statement that "light fasting can extend life", and there are no randomized trials on human mortality endpoints.
- **Applicable scope added**: People with diabetes, taking hypoglycemic drugs, and pregnant women may suffer from hypoglycemia if they miss a meal. The note says not applicable. Follow doctor’s advice.
