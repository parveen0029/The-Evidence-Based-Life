# Addendum: Voluntary Surrender, Confession, and Statute of Limitations (One Rule) · Verification Record (2026-09-21)

Task Origin: A user asked: "Should we add content on not harboring wishful thinking? In many criminal cases, defendants act out of wishful thinking — for instance, after a traffic collision, stopping first, helping victims, calling the police, and not fleeing." Handled under the "examples are not questions" protocol: the traffic collision rule already existed as Section 8 Rule 1; the actual question was whether the broader behavioral pattern of "wishful thinking" warranted a standalone rule.

**No generalized, abstract rule on "do not harbor wishful thinking" was added.** Rationale: It is neither quantifiable nor executable; drafting it would yield mere C-grade platitudes, clashing with the project rule that "every rule must answer what is spent and what is gained." Instead, wishful thinking was broken down into two objective decisions with explicit statutory numbers, synthesized into a single rule:

1. "Hide out first and see if anyone investigates" — Can you get away with it? Article 88 of the Criminal Law: where a case has been filed for investigation or accepted by a court, fleeing investigation or trial eliminates any statute of limitations protection.
2. "Will coming clean make things worse?" — Exactly how much sentencing mitigation is granted for voluntary surrender, truthful confession, and admission of guilt and acceptance of punishment? The sentencing guiding opinions issued by the Supreme Court and Supreme Procuratorate provide explicit statutory percentage reductions.

Placement: Added Section 8 Rule 6, inserted immediately after "hire a lawyer first when accused or summoned" (zero cost, large benefit; sequenced according to the section principle of "placing catastrophic yet easiest-to-execute safeguards first"). Original Rules 6 through 39 shifted to Rules 7 through 40.

Rated A: All three documents were verified verbatim and are currently effective. Benefit magnitude "Large", mechanically mapped to the freedom metric threshold (securing sentence reductions up to total exemption from punishment). Cost: Money=0, Time=Few; Perseverance is rated "Some" — the physical action is one-off (making a phone call or visiting a police station); the difficulty lies in suppressing the impulse to "wait and see" on the spot, rather than long-term habit resistance.

## Two Deliberate Methodological Trade-offs

1. **"The probability of being caught" was omitted**: While this represents the most direct counterweight to wishful thinking, clearance rates by crime category and hit-and-run detection rates in China lack verbatim-verifiable official statistical releases (mps.gov.cn presents persistent 521 errors). Resorting to secondary media reporting would violate project citation rules. This rule therefore anchors entirely on the "legal consequences" dimension, grounded in verifiable statutory provisions.
2. **Traffic accidents were not framed as "staying at the scene reduces your sentence"**: Fa Fa [2010] No. 60 explicitly states that protecting the scene, rescuing injured persons, and reporting to police are statutory obligations; even when surrender is recognized, leniency is strictly constrained. The genuine difference lies in the baseline sentencing bracket: staying is judged against the first tier of Article 133 of the Criminal Law, whereas fleeing before surrendering triggers the harsher tier as baseline. The remarks reflect this nuance without exaggerating the mitigation gained simply by "remaining at the scene."
