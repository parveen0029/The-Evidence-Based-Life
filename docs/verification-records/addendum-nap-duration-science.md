Task Origin: A reader asked: "Is there any content related to napping? Would you recommend adding it?"

Original Coverage: Searching the entire book for "nap," "power nap," "doze," "siesta" yielded only Section 3, Rule 11 ("Sleep for 10 minutes if drowsy in the afternoon; do not sleep for half an hour"), which operates under the time and mental energy metric, addressing solely alertness benefits and sleep inertia without touching on mortality. Remarks in Section 2, Rule 13 ("Sleep around 7 hours each night") noted that "higher risks with long sleep are mostly reverse causality," but that referred to nocturnal sleep without mentioning naps. Nap duration in relation to mortality and cardiovascular outcomes had zero coverage.

Placement: Added Rule 39 at the end of Section 2. Appending it rather than inserting it after Rule 13 (7 hours nocturnal sleep) avoided triggering rule renumbering cascades—Section 2 has 38 rules cited extensively across other sections, where insertion would require renumbering the whole section and manually auditing every citation, a risk unjustified by the return. ## Grading and Decisions

- **Graded B, not A**: Both primary pieces of evidence are observational studies recording natural habits without experimental allocation, carrying heavy reverse causation (sleep apnea, anemia, hypothyroidism, depression, and occult chronic disease all induce extended daytime sleep). The umbrella review synthesizes observational meta-analyses, and AMSTAR-2/GRADE ratings for individual outcomes were not provided in the abstract, failing to meet Grade A criteria of "quantifiable with rigorous study design."
- **Benefit rated "Medium", not "Large" by literal threshold**: Mortality metric thresholds designate relative reduction >=20% as "Large"; 30% for CHD and 13% per hour of nap duration literally qualify. Demoting to "Medium" is justified because these are observational correlations rather than intervention effects; Mendelian randomization supports only weak causality for blood pressure and waist circumference, failing to establish causal links to coronary disease or diabetes. Actual real-world benefits realized by readers will be far lower than raw correlational numbers. Documented here as "using judgment when numbers are insufficient."
- **Marked with "Controversy"**: Evidence from both sides is genuinely polarized—observational studies link long naps with elevated mortality and CHD, whereas genetic Mendelian randomization fails to find this causal chain. Both perspectives are transparently presented in the remarks.
- **Dashti's SD units not converted to mmHg**: The paper standardized variables using UK Biobank sample standard deviations (SBP 11.25 mmHg, DBP 20.65 mmHg); the DBP SD is visibly inflated while SBP is deflated, making converted mmHg numbers unreliable. The main text only notes directional trend ("slightly higher blood pressure and waist circumference") without figures.
- **The recommendation to "investigate underlying causes" is Grade C**: Nocturnal sleep apnea, anemia, hypothyroidism, and depression causing prolonged daytime drowsiness is clinical common sense, but "how much mortality drops after diagnosis" lacks citable intervention trials. Explicitly marked in remarks as author advice.
- **What wasn't written**: Whether naps can compensate for sleep lost the previous night—laboratory RCTs offer only short-term surrogate endpoints and duplicate Section 3, Rule 11, so excluded. Greek EPIC cohort (Naska 2007) suggested naps reduce coronary death, while Häusler 2019 suggested 1-2 naps weekly lowers risk; these represent opposite directions with small samples already covered by the umbrella review and were not listed separately.
- Beneficiary: Tier 1 (Self).
