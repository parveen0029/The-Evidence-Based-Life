# Addendum: Continuing School vs. Entering the Workforce After Middle School · Verification Record (2026-09-10)

Task Origin: A reader asked whether it is better to continue schooling or enter the workforce immediately after graduating from middle school, citing two common claims: "Some uneducated people become business owners" and "Some elite 985 graduates deliver takeout."

Original Coverage: Across the entire book, only Section 23, original Rule 1 (global return to education at 9%) was tangentially related, and it explicitly noted that "this is not a China-specific figure and can only serve as an order-of-magnitude reference." Searches for "child labor," "compulsory education," "financial aid / grants," "student loans," "secondary vocational school," and "Vocational Education Law" yielded zero hits across book/; "survivorship bias" appeared only once in the notes of Section 23, original Rule 6, referring to recruitment platform wage figures without covering educational attainment. In other words, the fundamental layer of "whether to continue studying" was a complete blank; the original Section 23 assumed readers had already decided to "learn something" and only addressed "what to study."

Placement: Added 6 new rules before Section 23, shifting original Rules 1 through 7 to Rules 7 through 13. The section intro was rewritten to: "The first six rules address whether to keep studying; the following seven rules address how to choose." Placing them in Section 23 rather than creating a new section was chosen because these six rules share the exact same evaluation metric with the subsequent seven rules (how much money and time spent vs. how much income returned); splitting them would require redundantly explaining the rate of return to education in both places. Entire book: 473 -> 479 rules; Grade A: 301 -> 306; Grade B: 123 -> 124; Grade C: unchanged at 49 rules; citation links: 918 -> 927; controversy annotations: 43 -> 44; TODOs: unchanged at 36.

## Rule 2 (Years of Education and Adult Mortality)

| URL | Verified | Source Excerpt |
|---|---|---|
| <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(23)00306-7> (Europe PMC REST retrieved citation and full abstract, PMID 38278172, The Lancet Public Health 2024;9(3):e155-e165) | Yes | "an average reduction in mortality risk of 1.9% (95% uncertainty interval 1.8-2.0) per additional year of education"; "2.9% (2.8-3.0) ... for adults aged 18-49 years, compared with a 0.8% (0.6-1.0) reduction for adults older than 70 years"; "603 of which were eligible for analysis and included data from 70 locations in 59 countries, producing a final dataset of 10 355 observations"; "We found no differential effect of education on all-cause mortality by sex or Socio-demographic Index level"; "We identified publication bias (p<0.0001)" |

Author attribution follows Europe PMC authorString as "IHME-CHAIN Collaborators", omitting individual first author. Grading: A (Controversy) - effect size, confidence intervals, and sample size are comprehensive, but authors self-reported publication bias and high heterogeneity; all included studies were observational, where education and family background confounding cannot be disentangled, which is explicitly noted in the remarks. Benefit magnitude: "Large" - applying thresholds via relative reduction under the mortality metric does not fit directly (1.9% annually is a single-year increment; completing high school vs. middle school adds three years, cumulatively ~5%-6%); here, it is judged Large based on "additional years of schooling typically count in three-year blocks, and reach 2.9% per year for ages 18-49," documented here. Costs: Money=0 (tuition accounted for separately in Rule 4), Time=High, Willpower=Yes, synthesizing into "Moderate" cost-effectiveness - this is honest: spending several more years in school is inherently one of the highest-cost actions in this book.
